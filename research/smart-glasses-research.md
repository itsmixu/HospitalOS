# Short, hands-busy moments win on glasses

The smart-glasses features that people use every day are short and transactional. They happen when the wearer's hands are busy or when taking out a phone would interrupt what they are doing. Examples are point-of-view (POV) capture, open-ear audio and calls, ten-second "what is this?" questions, translation subtitles, hearing a conversation in a noisy room, and above all reading and describing the world for blind and low-vision users. The heavily promoted always-on features have mostly not stuck: continuous Live AI, video calls on a 20° display, and turn-by-turn walking navigation. Past failures had different causes. Google Glass ran into social rejection of its camera. Humane, Rabbit and North Focals tried to replace the phone with hardware that was too immature and too expensive. Ray-Ban Meta succeeded with the opposite formula: it looks like normal glasses, costs about $300 and adds to the phone instead of replacing it. Meta's developer platform is now mature enough for a 36-hour build. Device Access Toolkit (DAT) 1.0.1 gives a native phone app the glasses' camera stream, microphone, speakers, device state and a 600×600 display card UI on Ray-Ban Display. Ray-Ban Display Web Apps get the display but cannot use the camera. Speech, inputs, motion and voice launch are still beta, and Bluetooth bandwidth limits how good the video can be. Past hackathon winners on Meta glasses share a profile: object memory, step verification or an assistive concept, demoed live on real hardware, with one hard number that proves it works. For a team new to glasses, the safest route is to fork Meta's CameraAccess sample. The best concept is an accessibility-first "look and ask" or "look and verify" loop, triggered by an event rather than recording continuously, that answers in under five seconds and treats consent, uncertainty and device-state handling as features to show in the demo.

## Seven million units sold, and the boring features carry them

Ray-Ban Meta/Oakley Meta sales show that the category now has a real market: **more than 7M units in 2025, over three times the ~2M sold across 2023–2024 combined** ([UploadVR](https://www.uploadvr.com/ray-ban-meta-glasses-sold-2-units-production-to-be-vastly-increased/)). Zuckerberg said **daily usage tripled year over year** but gave no per-feature breakdown ([UploadVR](https://uploadvr.com/meta-smart-glasses-daily-usage-tripled-year-over-year/)). Without vendor data, the best evidence for what people use comes from long-term reviewers, and they agree. A year-long user calls hands-free audio "the most consistent quality-of-life upgrade". Next come calls without earbuds, occasional POV photos and short "tell me what this is" questions. The same user reports vague AI answers in cluttered or dim scenes and pressure points after 30–60 minutes ([GeekChamp](https://geekchamp.com/what-ive-learned-after-nearly-a-year-of-wearing-ray-ban-meta-smart-glasses/)). Retail reviews name the camera as the most popular feature ([SlashGear](https://www.slashgear.com/1805909/are-ray-ban-meta-ai-glasses-worth-price-user-reviews)). Engadget's Gen 2 reviewer, by contrast, says: **"I still haven't found much use for Live AI"** ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/wearables/ray-ban-meta-2nd-gen-review-smart-glasses-are-finally-getting-useful-124720393.html)). The most notable new audio feature is Conversation Focus, which amplifies the person you are talking to. It scored a "B" in HearingTracker lab tests, though the testers say it is "not hearing aids" ([HearingTracker](https://www.hearingtracker.com/hearing-glasses/meta-ray-ban-glasses-in-hearadvisor-testing-how-do-they-perform-as-hearing-aids)).

The $799 Meta Ray-Ban Display shows the same pattern for screens. Engadget's favourite features were all glanceable: Instagram and WhatsApp messages as they arrive, a camera viewfinder that makes framing "way easier", Meta AI answers as swipeable cards that are easier to absorb than audio, and **translation shown as text, which felt "even more natural" than spoken translation**. Features that need sustained attention did poorly. Video calls felt "too cramped", walking navigation once sent the reviewer into an alley, and live captions were good for demos but had no everyday use for a hearing reviewer. The **69 g frame** slides down the nose and causes pressure after 6–7 hours. Display-heavy tasks drain the ~6 h battery much faster than that, and the display "is only compatible with a handful of apps" ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/wearables/meta-ray-ban-display-review-chunky-frames-with-impressive-abilities-193127070)). The Neural Band wristband is the input breakthrough. It "almost never missed a gesture" even with the hand in a pocket, which removes the social awkwardness of talking to your glasses in public (same source). Reviewers do note that it marks the skin and needs readjusting ([Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/meta-ray-ban-display-smart-glasses-review-is-this-the-future-we-really-want-2000679520)).

Other vendors confirm that the winning feature follows from the sensors a device has. Camera-free HUD glasses have found a niche in situations where looking at a phone is socially costly. TechRadar calls the Even Realities G2 teleprompter "the one feature that could potentially be the reason someone invests" ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/computing/virtual-reality-augmented-reality/even-realities-g2-review)). Even's Conversate app adds live transcription with summaries stored locally ([Even Realities](https://www.evenrealities.com/ja-AT/conversate)). The "memory AI" pitch, such as Brilliant Labs Halo's Narrative, has no review evidence yet that people keep using it ([Hackster](https://www.hackster.io/news/brilliant-labs-unveils-the-halo-smart-glasses-with-narrative-ai-and-vibe-mode-9755a165531d)).

The failures teach as much as the successes. Google Glass cost $1,500 and got its wearers banned from bars over recording fears, then retreated to enterprise use where stigma didn't matter ([Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/925830/wireless-how-google-glass-set-wearable-computing-back-10-years.html)). North's own staff called Focals clunky, rushed and of limited appeal to women and disabled users. Google bought North and shut Focals down in 2020 ([The Logic](https://thelogic.co/news/exclusive/north-staff-warned-ceo-its-smart-glasses-were-too-expensive-didnt-work-well-for-women/)). Humane's AI Pin overheated, cost too much and tried to replace the phone. HP bought the remains for ~$116M and the devices stopped working ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2025/02/19/humanes-ai-pin-is-dead-and-all-devices-will-stop-working-in-10-days)). Rabbit R1 often failed at the tasks it was sold for ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/computing/artificial-intelligence/with-the-humane-ai-pin-now-dead-what-does-the-rabbit-r1-need-to-do-to-survive)).

| Pattern | Evidence it works | Evidence it fails |
|---|---|---|
| POV capture, open-ear audio, calls | Most-used features in long-term reviews | Noisy low-light photos, thin audio at volume |
| One-shot "look and ask" | Daily use in reviews; core of blind users' routines | Vague answers in clutter; small text unreliable |
| Text cards and subtitles (Display) | Engadget's favourites; translation as text beats audio | None reported for short content |
| Continuous Live AI / always-on agents | Measured speed-ups in a lab study (VisionClaw) | Reviewers don't find a habit; privacy and battery cost |
| Video calls, Reels, games, navigation on 20° HUD | Demo appeal | "Too cramped"; misrouting |
| Phone-replacement gadgets | None | Humane, Rabbit, Focals shut down or stalled |

For the Junction brief, the lesson is to design for a moment that lasts under about ten seconds, in which the wearer's hands or eyes are busy and taking out a phone would interrupt the activity.

## Accessibility is the most proven first-person use case

Blind and low-vision users are the clearest group for whom a first-person camera beats a phone, and real institutions have built programmes around it. **Be My Eyes has run hands-free on Ray-Ban Meta since 13 November 2024**. Saying "Hey Meta, Be My Eyes" connects the wearer to a pool of about **8.1M sighted volunteers**, free of charge ([Be My Eyes](https://bemyeyes.com/blog/be-my-eyes-on-ray-ban-meta)). A May 2026 update added calls to trusted friends and family, a service directory with company representatives (Tesco, Amtrak, Hilton), and an action button that can be mapped to "describe what's around me" ([Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/05/meta-ai-wearables-changing-the-game-for-disabled-people/)). Meta's opt-in "detailed responses" setting, designed by a blind Meta engineer, overrides the default brevity for richer scene descriptions ([UploadVR](https://www.uploadvr.com/ray-ban-meta-glasses-meta-ai-detailed-responses-blind-low-vision/)). Third-party DAT apps already serve this group: OOrion finds saved objects, reads text and detects obstacles, and Aira connects to professional interpreters ([Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/05/meta-ai-wearables-changing-the-game-for-disabled-people/)). On adoption, RNIB offers try-before-you-buy fittings ([RNIB](https://shop.rnib.org.uk/blogs/news/try-ray-ban-meta-glasses-with-prescription-lenses-at-rnib)), the Blinded Veterans Association publishes a training guide ([BVA](https://bva.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Meta-Glasses-Training-Guide-for-Blind-and-Low-Vision-Users.pdf)), and a Canadian clinical trial (NCT07317180) is registered but not yet enrolling ([Veeva CTV](https://ctv.veeva.com/study/meta-glasses-for-low-vision)).

User accounts show what people actually do with the glasses. A totally blind user reads brochures, street and wall signs, licence plates and cooking instructions on packages "when I have nobody around to help me read or see things" ([ACB Next Generation](https://acbnextgeneration.org/?p=4269)). An Irish trainer tells similar tins apart, reads menus with prices, and pulls the one figure that matters from a bank letter. He also names the key failure mode: **"sometimes you'll ask Meta to read the text and instead it'll analyse it"**. He adds that answers depend on how the question is phrased, and that the glasses complement a cane or guide dog rather than replace it ([Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/technology/consumer-tech/2026/05/14/i-was-gobsmacked-smart-toy-brings-new-independence-for-the-visually-impaired/)). One user sums up the benefit: "no more trying to line a phone up with something" ([Be My Eyes](https://www.bemyeyes.com/?p=21193543)). Most of these tasks were already possible with a phone. **The gain comes from the form factor and the voice trigger, not new AI capability.**

Research explains how these tools fail and what designs fix it. An ACL 2025 study of 13 models, with tasks co-designed with blind people, found that **the hardest questions got the most confident answers**, and recommends rewarding abstention and prompting users to retake the photo ([ScienceNews.dk](https://www.sciencenews.dk/en/when-ai-sounds-certain-blind-users-have-to-trust-it-even-when-it-is-guessing)). In the ASSETS'24 study of 26 blind users, participants checked AI output by shaking cans, cross-checking apps and asking sighted people. They hit errors such as "dot com" read as "dot corn" and a white cane called a "hiking pole". They asked for directional camera guidance, blur warnings and corrections that persist ([Alharbi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06546v1)). Together these sources set a design standard. Read verbatim on request. Say when the model is uncertain, and abstain on safety-critical items such as medication, dates, money and door numbers. Guide the user to reframe the shot. Offer escalation from AI to a human. A blind editor whose glasses misread his own house number shows why that standard matters ([Tildes](https://tildes.net/~tech/1m2x/metas_ai_powered_ray_bans_are_life_enhancing_for_the_blind)).

Deaf and hard-of-hearing users have a crowded captioning-glasses market at $499–$799 (XRAI, AirCaps, Captify, LEION). Meta Ray-Ban Display is flagged as English-only and "not captioning-first" ([HearingTracker](https://www.hearingtracker.com/resources/hear-with-your-eyes-five-ar-live-captioning-glasses)). Captioning works one-on-one in quiet rooms. **Speaker attribution and sound direction are the unmet needs.** Deaf participants significantly preferred speech-bubble captions attached to speakers ([Peng et al.](https://pure.lib.cgu.edu.tw/en/publications/speechbubbles-enhancing-captioning-experiences-for-deaf-and-hard-/)) and valued seeing where a sound came from ([Jain et al.](https://soundability.eecs.umich.edu/img/portfolio/Jain_GlassEar_CHI2015.pdf)). Researchers also test feedback to the hearing partner when the Deaf partner is missing content ([Kang et al.](https://jennakangg.github.io/assets/pdfs/ChiCaptioning.pdf)). Meta extended Display captions to phone and messaging calls in May 2026 ([Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/05/meta-ai-wearables-changing-the-game-for-disabled-people/)).

Cognitive support has the strongest single result in the field. CrossSense, which won the £1m Longitude Prize on Dementia in March 2026, used glasses prompts to raise household-item naming from **46% to 82% in 23 dementia–carer pairs, holding at 78% an hour after removal** ([Resultsense](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-03-19-ai-smart-glasses-win-dementia-longitude-prize/)). The result is not yet peer-reviewed ([Optimist Daily](https://www.optimistdaily.com/2026/04/how-ai-powered-smart-glasses-could-transform-dementia-care-by-2027/)). The glasses ran about one hour on battery, cost up to £1,000, and drew ethical concern about data from vulnerable users (Resultsense). For motor impairments, voice capture is valued ("Being able to take them hands-free was incredible," says a veteran with quadriplegia), and Meta is researching Neural Band EMG with Carnegie Mellon for people with spinal-cord injury ([Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/05/meta-ai-wearables-changing-the-game-for-disabled-people/)). Independent testing with motor-impaired users is still missing. Evidence on older adults is thin. One study had just one participant over 65 ([Alharbi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06546v1)), and setup itself is a barrier: a Meta account and app are required, and no charging plug is included ([ACB Next Generation](https://acbnextgeneration.org/?p=4269)).

## DAT 1.0.1 gives you camera-in, audio-out and cards-on-display, with beta caveats

Meta's platform splits into two routes with very different abilities. The **Wearables Device Access Toolkit** is a native iOS/Android phone SDK, latest version 1.0.1 (9 October 2026), which fixed HEVC frame freezes and stream stutter ([Changelog](https://github.com/meta/meta-wearables-dat-android/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). Over Bluetooth it streams the camera at 2/7/15/24/30 fps and 360×640, 504×896 or 720×1280 resolution. It can capture photos mid-stream, play audio via A2DP (stereo out) or HFP (two-way mono voice, beamformed toward the wearer), and render a FlexBox UI of Text, Image, Button, Icon and small MP4 on the **600×600 Ray-Ban Display only** ([DAT overview](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/dat/build-overview); [Display overview](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/dat/display-overview)). Bandwidth is the main constraint. A stream drops resolution and then frame rate under load, and adaptive compression means **"requesting a lower resolution, a lower frame rate, or both can yield higher visual quality"** ([Streaming](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/dat/streaming)). Stream frames are cropped and stabilised, so they show a narrower view than standalone photos ([Camera stream processing](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/dat/camera-stream-processing)). That matters for reading small text, which was VisionClaw's weak point.

The 1.0 release added a set of features marked **dev and beta only**. They work in Developer Mode and private beta channels, which is enough for a hackathon, but they cannot ship to production. They are on-glasses speech recognition with partial and final transcripts, semantic inputs (touchpad, capture and action buttons, Neural Band swipes, select and drag), IMU motion data, standalone photos up to 4032×3024, in-stream PCM audio synced to video, and "Hey Meta, start <app>" voice launch ([Speech](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/dat/speech); [Inputs](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/dat/inputs); [Voice invocations](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/dat/voice-invocations)). Device state needs no session and maps directly onto the brief's "device state changes" requirement. It reports battery %, charging, worn or not worn, hinge open or closed, and an eight-level thermal reading ([Device state](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/dat/device-state)).

**Ray-Ban Display Web Apps** are ordinary HTTPS sites running in Chromium on the glasses. You connect one in Developer Mode by pasting a URL into the Meta AI app. Input arrives as arrow-key and Enter events from the touchpad and Neural Band. Web Apps get geolocation (through the phone), battery, device motion, offline Service Workers, limited text-to-speech, and WebMCP tools that Meta AI can call by voice within a 10 s timeout ([Web Apps overview](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/webapps/build/overview/); [Web Apps docs](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/webapps/)). The decisive limit is stated plainly: **"Only the Device Access Toolkit can use the glasses' camera or capture microphone audio"** ([llms.txt](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/llms.txt)). A Web App therefore cannot deliver the first-person field of view the brief centres on. It suits display-only or companion experiences. Black pixels render as transparent, text should be at least 16 px, JS must stay under 500 KB gzipped, and there is no on-device console.

| Capability | Ray-Ban Meta Gen 2 | Meta Ray-Ban Display |
|---|---|---|
| Camera stream / photo (DAT) | Yes | Yes |
| Mic and speakers (DAT) | Yes | Yes |
| Speech, Motion, Inputs, voice launch (beta) | Yes (speech presumed) | Yes (speech presumed) |
| Display UI (DAT DSL) | No | Yes, 600×600 |
| Neural Band input | No | Yes |
| Web Apps | No | Yes, no camera |

The required robustness behaviours in the brief map onto concrete DAT hooks. Permissions: registration is a one-time deep link into the Meta AI app. Camera permission is granted there (allow once, always, or deny), the microphone uses OS dialogs, and registration needs internet ([Permissions](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/dat/permissions-requests)). Errors and device state: StreamError covers HINGE_CLOSED, THERMAL_HOT, BATTERY_LOW, PEAK_POWER_LIMIT and TIMEOUT, and session errors include THERMAL_CRITICAL and BATTERY_CRITICAL ([StreamError](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/reference/android/dat/1.0/com_meta_wearable_dat_camera_types_streamerror)). Performance trade-offs: the resolution and fps ladder plus the documented advice to go lower. Meta's known-issues page lists the traps a demo will hit ([Known issues](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/knownissues)):

- speech silently stops after one minute;
- inputs die after the glasses are removed and put back on;
- beta capabilities do not resume after a pause;
- a double press emits a stray SHORT_PRESS first;
- display updates are batched during capture on Display;
- HFP must settle about 2 s before streaming starts, or audio fails silently;
- iPhone 17 on iOS 26 gets no HFP audio;
- **only one third-party app can be registered per pair of glasses in Developer Mode**.

Single tap and tap-and-hold on the touchpad are reserved by the system for pause and stop. Use double-tap or the capture button as triggers instead ([Inputs](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/dat/inputs)).

The proven AI architecture is simple. The glasses send camera and microphone data over Bluetooth to the DAT phone app. The app sends about **1 fps JPEG plus 16 kHz PCM** to a realtime multimodal model, or a single frame to a vision model over HTTPS. The reply comes back as speech through the glasses, and on Display as a text card. Google's reference build sends a 1 FPS track to Gemini Live through LiveKit ([DEV / Google AI](https://dev.to/googleai/realtime-multimodal-ai-on-ray-ban-meta-glasses-with-gemini-live-livekit-45mn)), and VisionClaw uses the same rate ([Suganthan](https://suganthan.com/blog/visionclaw-openclaw-meta-glasses/)). **No primary source publishes measured end-to-end latency, battery drain or time until thermal throttling.** Teams should measure these and present the numbers as part of the "performance trade-offs" requirement. Meta's CameraAccess sample, which has a built-in MockDeviceKit (MDK) toggle that uses the phone camera as a fake glasses camera, and its DisplayAccess sample are the intended starting points. The SDK repos ship Claude Code, Cursor and Codex plugins ([Mock Device Kit](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/dat/mock-device-kit); [AI-assisted dev](https://wearables.developer.meta.com/docs/develop/dat/ai-assisted)). MDK does not model stream and photo contention, so the team must validate on real hardware.

Its Meta prize was literally "Best Use of the Meta Wearables Device Access Toolkit Showcasing a Hands-free Everyday Use Case", nearly the Junction brief. **SmartSight co-won it with OurLife, and SmartSight also took Gold for Best in Hack** ([Reality Hack prizes](https://www.realityhackatmit.com/2026-tracks-prizes)). Neither project's description could be retrieved. The name suggests an assistive vision concept, but that is unconfirmed.

The research literature points the same way. VisionClaw, an always-on Gemini-plus-agent assistant on display-less Ray-Ban Meta, made tasks **13–37% faster and 7–46% less demanding** for 12 participants. Note-taking success fell to about 58%, however, because the camera struggled with small text such as receipts ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/always-on-ray-ban-meta-glasses-powered-by-openclaw-speed-up-everyday-tasks-in-new-study/)). Over 55 participant-days of field use, information retrieval (30%) and shopping (19%) led, and memory accounted for 12% ([arXiv 2604.03486](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.03486)). Ray-Ban Display HUD apps that drew press were narrow utilities. Ghost Run lets you race your past Strava run. Super, an agent, ordered Starbucks by voice ([UploadVR](https://uploadvr.com/meta-ray-ban-display-amazing-hud-apps/)). The Meta Connect Web Apps hackathon judged personal impact, whether the project works better on glasses than on a phone, and how quickly it delivers value ([Devpost](https://meta-connect-web-apps.devpost.com/)). No public record exists of past Junction Meta challenges.

The cautionary examples are just as clear. Harvard's I-XRAY piped Ray-Ban Meta video into PimEyes face search and LLM doxxing, and became the defining privacy scandal for the category ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/someone-put-facial-recognition-tech-onto-metas-smart-glasses-to-instantly-dox-strangers)). Reports of LED tampering ([OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents/2025-10-24-ad63)) and of subcontractors reviewing intimate visual queries ([UploadVR](https://uploadvr.com/subcontractors-see-intimate-meta-ai-visual-queries-from-smart-glasses)) added to the backlash. Road to VR called autonomous agents with account access "risky" ([Road to VR](https://www.roadtovr.com/clawdbot-ray-ban-smart-glasses-hack/)). **No winner found in this research documented an explicit consent mechanism.** That leaves room for a team that makes bystander-safe design part of its demo: capture triggered by an event, ephemeral processing, no face identification, and memory limited to the user's own objects and tasks. Apps that remember people sit uncomfortably close to I-XRAY. Apps that remember objects and verify tasks do not.

## Seven idea directions built on proven patterns

Each direction below is built on a pattern with evidence behind it, uses the camera's first-person view (which Web Apps cannot), and can be shown handling the brief's required states. The estimated 1–3 s vision round trip is an inference, not a measured figure, so every team should measure it on day one.

### 1. Verbatim-or-verify reader for safety-critical text

**Pattern:** Be My Eyes and Meta's look-and-ask, plus labellens, with the known failure fixed: AI summarises when it should read verbatim, and sounds confident when it is guessing. **Target user:** blind and low-vision people, and people with age-related macular disease reading medication labels, expiry dates, letters with money figures, and door or bus numbers. **Device:** Gen 2 first (audio only). Display adds a large-text card for low-vision users. **Build path:** fork CameraAccess. A capture-button press grabs a mid-stream photo, or a beta standalone 1080p photo for small text. Run an on-phone blur and framing check and give spoken "tilt down, move closer" guidance. A vision LLM call returns a verbatim transcription plus a self-rated confidence. Anything below threshold, or any medication, date or money field, triggers abstention ("I'm not sure, retake or call your contact"). The reply plays as TTS over A2DP. A one-press escalation sends the frame to a trusted contact, mirroring Be My Eyes' 2026 trusted-contact feature. **Pilot path:** RNIB-style low-vision centres, Finnish blindness associations, pharmacies. **Risks:** small-text OCR from glasses is the documented weak spot (VisionClaw's 58% note-taking success), so test on real labels early. Liability for medical misreads. It sits close to built-in Meta AI, so the differentiation must be the verbatim mode, abstention and escalation, shown explicitly.

### 2. Step-verified task coach for cognitive support

**Pattern:** Signoff's per-step photo verification combined with CrossSense's just-in-time prompts. **Target user:** people with early dementia, acquired brain injury or intellectual disability doing routine tasks such as a morning medication routine, making tea or using the washing machine, with a carer in the loop. A secondary audience is novices doing IKEA-style assembly. **Device:** Display is best, because step cards are the glanceable content reviewers liked. Gen 2 works with audio steps. **Build path:** a DAT app on Display using DisplayAccess-style step cards. The capture button or "Hey Meta, start <app>" begins a task. A photo at each step is judged pass, fail or unsure by a vision model. Repeated fails escalate to a notification to the carer. Report a Signoff-style accuracy number on a test set. **Pilot path:** memory clinics, care homes, Alzheimer's associations. CrossSense shows charities and prize funders will back this. **Risks:** consent from users with fluctuating capacity. Battery: CrossSense managed about one hour. Mis-verification that leads to unsafe actions, so keep the verified tasks low-stakes in the demo. The Display back event is not delivered on real glasses, so design navigation without it.

### 3. Own-object memory ("where did I leave it?") with no faces and ephemeral storage

**Pattern:** Yaad, OOrion's find-saved-objects, OpenGlass "what did I see earlier", realityos-spatial-memory. These are recurring hackathon winners. **Target user:** people with memory impairment and ADHD, blind users locating dropped items, and everyone who loses keys. **Device:** Gen 2 or Display. On Display the answer can include the stored thumbnail. **Build path:** DAT stream at 2 fps, low resolution. Detect objects on the phone against a user-enrolled list (keys, wallet, pill box, glasses case). Store only matched sightings with timestamp and place label, and blur or drop frames containing faces. Answer "where are my keys?" by voice from the sighting log. Pause automatically on doff or hinge close, and back off on thermal warnings. **Pilot path:** occupational therapists, ADHD coaching services. **Risks:** reviewers don't keep using continuous capture (Live AI), and continuous streaming costs battery and heat, so consider capture only on the capture button or at "set-down" moments. Bystander privacy needs to be visible in the pitch. Recognising small objects at 360p may fail.

### 4. Speaker-aware captions with partner feedback

**Pattern:** the captioning-glasses market plus Speechbubbles (speaker attribution) and Kang et al. (partner feedback), targeting the gaps that commercial products paywall or leave unsolved. **Target user:** Deaf and hard-of-hearing people in small-group conversations. **Device:** Display. **Build path:** a DAT app on Display. Use the beta in-stream camera audio, which is not beamformed and so captures other people, unlike HFP. Send it to a cloud streaming ASR with diarization. Render captions labelled by speaker, and use the low-fps camera to show which visible person is talking. A phone screen facing the partner shows "slow down / repeat" cues. **Pilot path:** hard-of-hearing associations, university disability services. **Risks:** Meta Display already captions in English, so differentiate on speaker labels, multilingual support or partner feedback. Mono audio cannot give sound direction (an inference from the documented audio paths). Caption latency must keep pace with speech, and on-glasses speech stops after one minute, so use cloud ASR. Running audio and video together raises bandwidth, heat and battery load. Group conversations in noise are the known hard case.

### 5. Look-and-translate for everyday Finnish paperwork and signs

**Pattern:** reviewers found text translation on Display "even more natural" than audio, combined with look-and-ask. **Target user:** newcomers and international students in Finland handling pharmacy labels, Kela letters, shop signs and menus. **Device:** Display (subtitle card), with Gen 2 as the audio fallback. **Build path:** a capture-button photo goes to a vision LLM that both translates and explains ("this is a prescription refill notice; deadline 20 Nov"), returned as one card with an optional deeper "explain" step. Cache recent translations for offline recall, and handle loss of connectivity with a clear "saved, will translate when online" state. **Pilot path:** city integration services, university international offices. **Risks:** it overlaps Meta's built-in translation (check Finnish support on site). The same small-text reliability problem as idea 1. Lower novelty unless the explanation layer clearly adds value.

### 6. Look-and-pinch control for people with limited dexterity

**Pattern:** smart-home control by Neural Band swipes (UploadVR's HUD roundup), lookgrab's look-then-pinch, Meta's EMG accessibility research, and voice capture valued by a user with quadriplegia. **Target user:** wheelchair users and people with limited hand function or dysarthria. **Device:** Display plus Neural Band. **Build path:** a DAT app identifies the device in view (a lamp or TV marked with a fiducial or recognised by a vision model) and shows a confirm card. A Neural Band select or capture-button press toggles it through a home-automation API. Voice, button and band are all supported so that no single input method is required. **Pilot path:** spinal-injury rehab centres, assisted-living housing. **Risks:** DAT exposes only semantic Neural Band events, not raw EMG. The band ships only with Display. Smart-home integration eats hackathon hours. Inputs stop after doff and don, so re-add them on don-state changes.

### 7. Hands-busy cue cards via a Web App and WebMCP (lowest-risk fallback)

**Pattern:** the Even G2 teleprompter and AugmentedChords: text in view while hands and eyes stay on the task. **Target user:** presenters, musicians, cooks following a recipe, and people with speech anxiety or aphasia rehearsing scripts. **Device:** Display, Web App. **Build path:** a static site on Vercel. Neural Band arrow keys page through cards, WebMCP tools let users say "next" or "go to step 4", and a Service Worker gives offline use. A working version takes hours. **Pilot path:** a speaker-coaching or aphasia therapy group. **Risks:** **it does not use the camera**, so it is weak on the brief's "first-person field of view" criterion, which makes it a fallback or a companion layer to a DAT app. WebMCP is off by default and has a 10 s tool timeout.

For a first-time team, ideas 1 and 2 offer the best mix of proven demand, Meta's own partner priorities, and a demo that can show permissions, uncertainty, device state and consent end to end.

## Conclusion

The evidence changes what a strong entry looks like. The winning move is not a more capable always-on assistant, because reviewers, failed gadgets and the privacy backlash all point away from that. It is a narrow first-person loop with a clear trigger and an answer in seconds. The technical difference from Meta's own features comes from what Meta AI handles poorly: reading verbatim, admitting uncertainty, handing off to a human, and checking a step instead of describing a scene. The brief's robustness checklist, consent requirement and inclusive-design criterion can be shown as features rather than treated as overhead. A demo that visibly pauses on doff, backs off on a thermal warning, abstains on a blurry pill label and never stores a face shows more design maturity than any 2024–2026 winner documented.

Three unknowns should drive the first day of work. No source measures end-to-end latency, battery drain or thermal time until throttling on DAT, so the team that measures and reports these turns a gap in the evidence into a strength. Small-text reading from the glasses camera is the documented weak link, so validate it on the real hardware before committing to a reading-based idea. Developer Mode allows only one registered app per pair of glasses, so team logistics around the 20 shared devices matter as much as code.
